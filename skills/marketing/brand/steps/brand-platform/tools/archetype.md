# Archetype

Fixed text of session 1 of 4 of the brand platform. Show `Briefing` word for word; ask the `Dig` questions as written; show the `Sharpness tests` as a checklist; say the `Link` as written. When the brand already has a name there is no Name session: say "of 3" instead of "of 4".

## Briefing

**Strategist:** Session 1 of 4: the **Archetype**.

**Where it comes from.** The twelve brand archetypes of Margaret Mark and Carol Pearson, from their book "The Hero and the Outlaw", 2001. They built on Carl Jung's observation that the same few characters return in the stories of every culture.

**The idea.** People recognize a character faster than they follow an argument. A brand that plays one character, the same one every time, is understood before it explains anything. There are twelve:

Innocent  - Promises that life can be simple and good. - Coca-Cola
Everyman  - Belongs with ordinary people and asks for no status. - IKEA
Hero      - Proves worth by overcoming what is hard. - Nike
Outlaw    - Breaks the rules that no longer serve people. - Harley-Davidson
Explorer  - Leaves the known to find something truer. - Patagonia
Creator   - Makes things of lasting value from imagination. - LEGO
Ruler     - Brings order and sets the standard others follow. - Rolex
Magician  - Turns a vision into a changed reality. - Disney
Lover     - Makes people feel wanted and close. - Chanel
Caregiver - Protects people and looks after them. - Johnson & Johnson
Jester    - Makes the moment lighter and tells the truth by joking. - Old Spice
Sage      - Seeks the truth and shares what it finds. - Google

The examples are the common reading of the trade; not all of them are from the book.

**Why now.** The Brand Wheel gave your brand its traits. The archetype gives those traits one face. The voice and the look are both cast from it.

**What we will leave with.** A primary archetype, a secondary one that colors it, and the headline: the **Character**, one line that begins "The ... who ...".

## Dig

Before asking, show `Personality` from the Brand Wheel and say that these traits are the starting point.

1. In your customer's story, who is the brand: the one who leads, the one who stands beside them, or the one who shows another way?
2. What is the brand against? Name it: a habit, a fear, a kind of company.
3. Who does your customer want to become by choosing you?
4. Which characters do your competitors play, and which one is nobody playing?
5. Take each trait from the Brand Wheel. Where would it go wrong with your customer? Finish the line: "..., but not ...".

## Sharpness tests

- At most one competitor from the Positioning plate plays the primary archetype.
- The secondary changes how the primary behaves; it is not a second primary.
- The Character could not be said of a competitor.
- Each "but not" names a way the trait really fails with your customer.
- Nothing contradicts the Essence.

## Link

Next: the Voice. You know who the character is; next we hear how it talks.

## Notes for the agency

- `Primary archetype` and `Secondary archetype` are each one of the twelve names of the briefing, and they differ.
- After the last Dig question, and before the minting, propose three pairs of a primary and a secondary archetype. For each pair say how the brand would sound and look, and whether a competitor from the Positioning plate already plays it. A pair that most competitors play is a weak choice: say so aloud. The CEO picks the pair; only then does the Copywriter mint the `Character`.
- Draft the answer to question 4 from the Positioning plate first and ask the CEO to correct it.
- `Character` is one line of up to 12 words in the form "The ... who ...".
- `Why this pair` is one line of up to 20 words: what the customer needs from the brand and what the alternatives are not.
- `Personality` is 3 to 5 lines, each "Trait, but not X: what it means in practice". The traits start from `Personality` in the Brand Wheel; dig only the "but not" half.
- The headline slot is `Character`.
